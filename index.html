Introduction

Getting to know
the service.

Donkey Republic is a Danish company, founded in 2014, that runs app-based bike and e-bike sharing. The idea is a more sustainable way to get around a city, and an alternative to driving, taking public transport, or owning a bike you have to store and maintain yourself. Today the service runs across eight European countries, with electric bikes available in selected cities.

Over seven weeks, our team of four took a close look at how the service actually works, where it frustrates riders, and whether Donkey Republic could get more of them to subscribe instead of paying ride by ride. We approached each question with a different service design tool, and this case study is built around them. Coming from an IT and business background, I was often the one in the team digging into what each tool was for and how to use it well.

A detail we liked: the company gives something back to the animal it's named after. All the revenue from rides on two special bikes goes to the Silver Star Lamu Donkey Care Project in Kenya.

How it works
Find and unlock a bike through the app, ride, then leave it at a hub. Both regular bikes and e-bikes are available, on a membership or pay-as-you-go.
The brief
Improve internal operations across every city, and build deeper customer loyalty, with a particular focus on subscription models.
Our lens
A sequence of service design tools, each one building on what the last uncovered.

01 · Service Blueprint · Group

The membership ride,
end to end.

We started from the customer's point of view and mapped the full journey of a rider who already holds a membership. We chose a member on purpose, since they use more of the service than a one-time rider, which gave us a fuller journey to work with. Working in Figma, we laid out the customer actions and touchpoints first, then the frontstage, and finally the trickier part, the invisible backstage that keeps everything running.

Misplaced & crowded bikes

The clearest pain points were bikes left in the wrong places and parking hubs that get overcrowded. Both come down to relocating the fleet more often, guided by which locations riders actually use.

The app is a single point of failure

Every step depends on the app, so a slow connection or a Bluetooth glitch at the lock can leave a rider stuck at the bike, unable to start or finish a trip.

Membership needs a rewrite

The membership tiers were confusing about what each one actually includes. Clearer descriptions could win over riders stuck between pay-as-you-go and subscribing, and keep them around longer.

Methods & key sources
  • Figma: a broad Service Blueprint template, chosen for clarity and easy modification.
  • ChatGPT: feedback on the trickier backstage and invisible actions, which we then discussed and reworked ourselves.
  • Our own research into the backstage processes, alongside reasoning by analogy with comparable services (e.g. Alepa Fillari) and public customer feedback.

02 · Strategy Explorer · Group

The subscription
question.

We based the Strategy Explorer on Donkey Republic's annual and quarterly reports to get a clear picture of where the company stands today. From there, the more useful question was where it could go next, so we focused the exercise on one thing: how could Donkey Republic build stronger engagement and retention through subscriptions?

More than a price

The map kept pointing back to the same thing: subscriptions aren't only a pricing decision. Whether someone commits comes down to experience, trust, and a service that behaves consistently, which makes it a design problem as much as a commercial one.

Strengths

  • A recognisable, well-defined service concept
  • Existing partnerships with cities
  • A sustainability-oriented brand
  • A clear role in urban mobility

Weaknesses

  • App stability
  • Unclear membership benefits
  • Fleet reliability
  • Service consistency across cities

Opportunities

  • Clearer subscription tiers
  • Stronger incentives for regular riders
  • Corporate memberships & meaningful partnerships
  • Improved fleet maintenance & a more stable app

Trends & directions

  • Multimodal links to buses and other transport
  • Health & wellbeing features tied to riding data
  • Subscriptions as experience, not just pricing
  • Trust, loyalty & consistency as design problems

Where we'd act

01

Redesign membership tiers so their value is obvious before committing.

02

Reward regular riders with incentives that make subscribing worthwhile.

03

Invest in app stability, the backbone of the whole experience.

04

Improve fleet maintenance and expand e-bikes in high-traffic areas to keep availability high.

05

Build meaningful partnerships, from corporate memberships to rider-wellbeing tie-ups.

06

Explore multimodal and health-linked features as future differentiators.

Where it landed

Pulling the map together, the strategy came down to a few connected moves: make the membership tiers and pricing genuinely easy to understand, give companies a reason to put their staff on the service, and keep enough e-bikes in the busy parts of the city that a member is never left waiting. The ambition behind all of it is for Donkey Republic to become the bike-share people across Northern Europe actually rely on.

Methods & key sources
  • Strategy Explorer template, mapped in Figma.
  • Donkey Republic annual & quarterly reports.
  • Trend analysis connecting Assignment 1's pain points to broader strategy.

03 · Personas & Value Proposition Canvas · Group

Two personas.
Does the offer fit?

To keep the value canvas grounded in real, specific situations, we built two personas first: a recently retired first-time e-bike user, and a family of four who'd just moved to the city. We then mapped each one against the Strategyzer Value Proposition Canvas to see where Donkey's offer fits their needs, and where it doesn't.

Keeping the personas honest

The personas began as AI drafts, and one detail didn't hold up. The model assumed the app defaults to Danish, but the live app simply follows the phone's language setting, so the language barrier we'd written in wasn't real. We cut it. A small fix, but it set the rule for the rest of the work: treat an AI persona as a fast first draft, not the final word, and check every claim against how the product actually behaves before letting it steer a design decision.

04 · AI Landscape & Functionality Cards · Group

Finding where
AI fits.

Using the Oblo AI Landscape Canvas, we ran a workshop to find the places where AI could actually make a difference across the service lifecycle. We rebuilt the service in post-its, covering searching, unlocking, riding, returning, paying and support, plus the maintenance teams and suppliers behind it. Then we drew the dependencies between frontstage and backstage and matched the problems we'd found to the canvas's functionality cards.

Back-end operations

  • Entering and managing markets across different countries
  • Resource optimisation for distributed maintenance
  • Inaccurate stock, supplier and cost forecasting
  • Keeping quality standards consistent per market

Front-end delivery

  • Unlocking issues and inconsistent GPS / location
  • Riders who prefer talking to support over text
  • Accessibility across age groups and languages
  • Unfamiliarity with local riding rules

The two we'd build first · functionality cards

Priority · Theme 07: Accessibility

A multimodal AI assistant

Some riders would rather call support; others would rather type. So we proposed a multimodal assistant that brings together speech recognition, language understanding and audio output: a rider can simply talk to it, get a reply in voice or text, and have it carry out actions directly in the app. It makes the service more inclusive and lets support scale across different languages and age groups.

Multimodal user interfacesAudio processingNatural language processingBots, agents & assistants
Priority · Theme 02: Operations

Real-time maintenance decision-support

A decision-support system that pulls together bike usage, breakdowns, stock levels, supplier lead times and price trends, then recommends where to send maintenance crews, which parts to order, and how to prioritise repairs. The aim is to cut downtime and cost while keeping availability and quality high in every market.

Resource optimizationPredictive modelingAnomaly detectionProcess automation
How we worked

Because so many of the problems were connected, we built small chains of problem-and-solution post-its, where solving one thing sometimes created a new challenge that another functionality card could pick up. That kept the ideas connected rather than scattered, and helped us cut a long list of options down to two that felt both realistic and high-impact.

Methods & key sources
  • AI Landscape Canvas, mapped in Figma, using its AI opportunity cards (prediction, automation, assistance, optimization, multimodal interaction).
  • No AI models were run in this exercise; we combined the canvas with service knowledge from earlier assignments.

Who holds power,
and who holds interest?

For the individual assignment I picked a tool the group hadn't used, the Power-Interest Matrix, and placed Donkey Republic's stakeholders according to how much power and how much interest each one holds in the service. It's a tool I'd reach for right at the start of a project, when you're deciding whose needs to research and who to bring into co-design sessions.

The takeaway

Mapping the stakeholders myself first, then widening the list through AI-assisted brainstorming, surfaced four I'd skipped: payment and insurance providers, transport regulators, and potential B2B clients. Placing everyone by power and interest made the priority obvious. The high-power, high-interest corner, where the relationships matter most, held municipalities, users, the internal team, maintenance teams and transport regulators. Of those, municipalities stood out: they control permits, parking and public space, so they can expand the service or shut it down. The clearest conclusion was that Donkey Republic should invest most in its municipal relationships, something the group had touched on but the matrix made impossible to ignore.

Methods & key sources
  • Power-Interest Matrix for stakeholder mapping, mapped in Figma.
  • Stakeholders brainstormed independently, then expanded with two AI tools (Claude and ChatGPT).

Reflection

What the tools
showed.

The five tools leaned on each other more than expected. The blueprint named the symptoms. The Strategy Explorer turned those into a direction. The personas and value canvas explained why each problem mattered to a real rider. The AI landscape pointed to what was worth building first. And the Power-Interest Matrix showed who has the most influence over where it goes next.

One finding kept surfacing across the tools: the subscription question is really a service question. Whether someone commits depends on the app being reliable, the membership being easy to understand, and a bike being there when they need it, far more than on the price. That points to where the design work should go first: the membership itself. A simple click-through of the sign-up flow, tried with riders like these two personas, would show whether they can understand the tiers and pick one without second-guessing.
